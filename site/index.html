What the terms mean

Land is protected in two basic ways. With fee simple, the public buys and owns the land, so you can often visit it. With an easement, the land stays private and often closed to the public, but it can never be developed.

Holding type

Fee Simple
The county or a partner agency buys the land outright and holds the title. The public owns and manages it, and it is often open for recreation. This is the most expensive way to protect land.
Conservation Easement
The owner keeps the land but sells or donates the right to develop it. A permanent deed restriction bars subdividing and building, and the land stays private, usually with no public access. It costs far less per acre than buying the land. That is why these records often list a private owner.
Working Forest & Farmland
Protection for land that stays in timber or agricultural production. The owner keeps farming or harvesting under a management plan but can't develop the land.

Conservation land parcels: use

Public Conservation Land
Land a government agency owns outright for conservation.
Private property encumbered by a public conservation or agricultural easement
Private land where a government agency holds an easement that limits development (or, for an agricultural easement, keeps the land in farming).
Regulatory conservation easement
An easement required as a condition of a development permit, often to protect wetlands, rather than one that was bought.
Privately held conservation easement (non-regulatory)
An easement held by a private group, such as a land trust, rather than a government.
Mitigation bank (public or private)
Land that is restored or preserved to generate credits, which developers buy to offset wetlands they destroy elsewhere. The land is protected as a regulatory offset, not as a conservation purchase.
Private Conservation Land
Land a private conservation group owns outright.
